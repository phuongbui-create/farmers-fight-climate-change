"Sustainable" means the body can shed all the heat it makes. Above that level, work is still possible in short bouts with cool-down breaks, but heat builds up in the body during each bout.

What's the issue?

Working muscles produce heat, and the body has to get rid of it. On a hot day most of that heat leaves through sweat evaporating from the skin. That can fail in two ways, which are the two failure modes on the course slides. In humid air, sweat can't evaporate fast enough. In very hot, dry air, the body can't make sweat fast enough. When either limit is reached, the body stores heat and core temperature rises. The harder someone works, the sooner that happens, so the real question for a farmer isn't "is it hot?" but "how hard can my crew work today without overheating?"

That's measured in METs (metabolic equivalents). Sitting is about 1.5, housework about 3, brisk walking about 4, dancing about 5, and heavy lifting about 7. The planner finds the highest MET level at which a body can still shed all the heat it makes.

What the controls represent

  • Air temperature (70–120 °F, about 21–49 °C): the forecast shade temperature. The range runs from a mild morning to near-record U.S. heat.
  • Relative humidity (5–100 %): desert afternoons are near 10 %, and humid Gulf Coast mornings are near 100 %.
  • Wind speed (0–25 mph): the forecast wind. The model assumes the air at body height moves at 70 % of the forecast speed.
  • Worker and exposure: the four panels on the course slides. Older adults sweat less, and full sun adds radiant heat. As slide 3 notes, sun moves every limit a few degrees cooler, and age moves it further.

The defaults (95 °F / 35 °C, 40 % humidity) are the "Sept. 21, 2026 at 4 pm" example from the course slides, a typical College Station afternoon. A light breeze of 6 mph, sun and an 18–40-year-old worker match panel c.

What to notice as you move the sliders

  • Humidity and temperature trade off. The color bands on the map slope down to the right: on a dry day you can handle much higher temperatures than on a humid one. The bands follow wet-bulb temperature on the humid side but bend on the dry side, where the sweat-rate limit takes over.
  • Wind's effect depends on the other two sliders. At 95 °F and 40 % humidity, wind raises safe effort because it speeds up evaporation. Raise the temperature to about 105 °F or more with low humidity, and the wind line rises for the first few mph, then slopes down: the air is hotter than skin (about 96 °F), so extra wind blows more heat onto the body, and the worker is already sweating as fast as possible.
  • Age and sun shift everything. Toggle the worker and exposure buttons and watch the livable area shrink. At the default conditions, a young worker in shade can sustain about 4 METs (brisk walking). In sun that drops to about 3.2 (housework), and an older worker in sun manages only about 2.4.
  • Below 1.5 METs the conditions aren't livable, even sitting still. Near College Station, the highest wet-bulb temperature observed in August from 2017 to 2026 was 28.1 °C (82.6 °F, 9 Aug 2024; course slide 10), short of the 31 °C line but close enough to cut safe work sharply.

How it's calculated

  1. A steady-state heat balance for a lightly clothed worker with skin at 35.5 °C: metabolic heat − breathing losses + dry heat gain from hot air + solar gain = evaporative cooling.
  2. Evaporative cooling is limited by the air (vapor-pressure difference between skin and air, times an evaporation coefficient that grows with wind speed) or by the body (maximum sweat rate, lower for older adults), whichever is smaller. These are the two failure modes on slide 3.
  3. We solve for the largest metabolic rate that balances, then divide by 58.2 W/m² to get METs.
  4. Wet-bulb temperature uses Stull's (2011) formula.

This is a simplified version of the Vanos et al. model. Its clothing, sweat-rate and solar parameters were tuned so the livability limits match the course figures (slides 3, 9 and 10) within about 1–2 °C. It's a teaching tool, not a medical device. Watch workers for signs of heat illness whatever the numbers say.
